### 1 Kings 13:11–14 · Who mounts the donkey?

**Keep for this bank.** The sons prepare the animal and their father rides it. Verse 13 changes from plural 'they' to singular 'he'. 'For me' helps identify the rider, but the reader still has to assign the two actions to the right people. The short question tests that cleanly.

**Key** — the old prophet  
**Competitor** — his sons

> **11** Now there was a certain old prophet living in Bethel, whose sons came and told him all that the man of God had done there that day. They also told their father what he had said to the king.
> **12** Their father asked them, “Which way did he go?” And his sons showed him which road the man of God from Judah had taken.
> **13** So he said to his sons, “Saddle the donkey for me.” And when they had saddled the donkey for him, he mounted it
> **14** and rode after the man of God. He found him sitting under an oak tree and asked, “Are you the man of God who came from Judah?” “I am,” he replied.

### 1 Kings 13:18–19 · According to the claim, who is to eat and drink?

**Remove for this bank.** The man of God is a supported key. The stem makes the reader identify what 'the claim' refers to inside nested reported speech, yet verse 19 gives the same person simply by saying who ate. A question about what the old prophet claimed would make this distinction worth testing.

**Key** — The man of God.  
**Competitor** — The old prophet.

> **18** The old prophet answered, “I too am a prophet, as you are. And an angel said to me by the word of the Lord: ‘Bring him back with you to your house so that he may eat bread and drink water.’” (But he was lying to him.)
> **19** So the man of God returned with him and ate and drank in his house.

### Acts 23:19–22 · What must happen before the ambushers act?

**Keep for this bank.** The ambushers are already in position. The young man says exactly what they are still waiting for: the commander's consent. This distinguishes approval of the request from Paul actually arriving before the council. 'Act' could be tightened, but 'ready now, waiting for' resolves the intended stage.

**Key** — The commander’s consent to their request.  
**Competitor** — Paul being brought before the Sanhedrin.

> **19** The commander took the young man by the hand, drew him aside and asked, “What is it you want to tell me?”
> **20** He said: “Some Jews have agreed to ask you to bring Paul before the Sanhedrin tomorrow on the pretext of wanting more accurate information about him.
> **21** Don’t give in to them, because more than forty of them are waiting in ambush for him. They have taken an oath not to eat or drink until they have killed him. They are ready now, waiting for your consent to their request.”
> **22** The commander dismissed the young man with this warning: “Don’t tell anyone that you have reported this to me.”

### Acts 23:27–30 · Who is ordered to present a case to the addressee?

**Remove for this bank.** Verse 30 explicitly supplies 'his accusers'. The stem turns the writer's perfectly ordinary 'you' into 'the addressee' and then follows the wording of that same clause. It tests familiarity with a letter-writing term more than the reader's understanding of the order.

**Key** — his accusers  
**Competitor** — the Sanhedrin

> **27** This man was seized by the Jews and they were about to kill him, but I came with my troops and rescued him, for I had learned that he is a Roman citizen.
> **28** I wanted to know why they were accusing him, so I brought him to their Sanhedrin.
> **29** I found that the accusation had to do with questions about their law, but there was no charge against him that deserved death or imprisonment.
> **30** When I was informed of a plot to be carried out against the man, I sent him to you at once. I also ordered his accusers to present to you their case against him.

### Judges 9:21–25 · Why does Jotham live elsewhere?

**Remove for this bank.** Verse 21 gives Jotham's move and fear of his brother in a single because-clause. The Shechemites appear in the subsequent conflict with Abimelek. The window gives no comparable reason to think Jotham moved because he feared them. This is a sound basic comprehension question with little discriminating value here.

**Key** — Because he fears his brother Abimelek.  
**Competitor** — Because he fears the citizens of Shechem.

> **21** Then Jotham fled, escaping to Beer, and he lived there because he was afraid of his brother Abimelek.
> **22** After Abimelek had governed Israel three years,
> **23** God stirred up animosity between Abimelek and the citizens of Shechem so that they acted treacherously against Abimelek.
> **24** God did this in order that the crime against Jerub-Baal’s seventy sons, the shedding of their blood, might be avenged on their brother Abimelek and on the citizens of Shechem, who had helped him murder his brothers.
> **25** In opposition to him these citizens of Shechem set men on the hilltops to ambush and rob everyone who passed by, and this was reported to Abimelek.

### Judges 9:11–12 · Why does the fig tree not want to rule the trees?

**Remove for this bank.** The fig tree answers with a rhetorical question. A reader must infer that ruling would mean giving up its sweet fruit and that this cost explains its refusal. Naming fruit alone would be an incomplete answer. The later invitation to the vine is a weak distractor, but the open question and key still test a meaningful inference. The original Judges 9:9–10 window uses the same rhetorical tradeoff for the olive tree. The olive gives up oil by which gods and humans are honored, whereas the fig gives up its sweet fruit. Both questions test the same inference in adjacent stanzas. The olive question additionally asks the reader to recover why the oil matters. I would spend one slot on the olive question. If that item were absent, I would keep the fig question.

**Key** — it would have to lose its sweet fruit  
**Competitor** — because the trees turned to the vine

> **11** “But the fig tree replied, ‘Should I give up my fruit, so good and sweet, to hold sway over the trees?’
> **12** “Then the trees said to the vine, ‘Come and be our king.’

**Parallel item · Judges 9:9–10** — Why does the olive tree refuse to take power over the other trees?  
**Key** — Because it would have to give up its oil that honors gods and humans.

> **9** “But the olive tree answered, ‘Should I give up my oil, by which both gods and humans are honored, to hold sway over the trees?’
> **10** “Next, the trees said to the fig tree, ‘Come and be our king.’
